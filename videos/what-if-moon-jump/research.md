# What if your Moon jump went too high? — research

On the Moon you weigh about a sixth of what you weigh on Earth, so the same push off the ground sends you far
higher, and you hang in the air longer. What the Moon does not cut is mass: the suit and the life-support backpack
still carry all of theirs, and once that load starts to tip, it keeps going. On April 1972's Apollo 16, at the end of
the third moonwalk, Charlie Duke and John Young held a "Moon Olympics" (the Munich Olympics were coming that year).
Young jumped first; Duke made "a big push off the moon, getting about four feet high", and as he straightened up
"the weight of my backpack pulled me over backward". He could not correct it and fell the four feet "right on my
backpack". The backpack was a fiberglass shell holding all his life-support systems: had it broken or the suit split,
he would have lost his air. Young's only comment on the loop was "Charlie! ... That ain't very smart." Young helped
him up; Duke stood listening for damaged pumps and checking for a leak, heard nothing, and stayed grounded for the
rest of the walk. The crews' real way of moving was a low lope or skip; the two-footed kangaroo hop was used on level
ground only in play.

## Left out

- **Who fell:** IFLScience credits the jump and fall to John Young. That is a misattribution: the ALSJ page is an
  excerpt of Duke's own book *Moonwalker* (1990) in his voice, and the transcript has Young calling "Charlie!". Duke it is.
- **Suit and backpack weights in pounds:** sources range from 170 to 200 lb on Earth (and ~30 lb on the Moon) with no
  agreed split between suit and backpack; Duke's own "weighed as much as I did" is used instead.
- **CapCom Tony England's "For a 380-pound guy":** a joke in the loop, not a measurement.
- **Escape speed, comet jumps (Philae's bounce):** off the story; Philae's 1 km bounce height is unconfirmed.
- **Exact date of the third moonwalk:** sources give April 23 or 24 (time zones, timelines); the script says only
  "April 1972".
- **Fall speed or "equivalent Earth fall" numbers:** our own arithmetic, not sourced; left out.
- **"One second to panic":** the ALSJ page title, not a measured duration.
