# Research: Percy Spencer and the melted candy bar that became the microwave oven

## Mechanism

A magnetron is the vacuum tube at the heart of a radar set: it turns electricity into microwaves, radio waves a few
centimetres long. Percy Spencer, a self-taught engineer who never finished grammar school and who had worked out how
to make magnetrons by the thousand for wartime radar, was standing next to a running one when a chocolate bar in his
pocket melted. Others had felt the warmth before; he was the one who tested it, first with popcorn kernels, which
popped all over the lab, then with an egg, which burst. Microwaves make the water (and fat and sugar) molecules in
food twist back and forth to follow the wave; the friction between molecules is heat, so the food warms from inside
its first centimetre with no flame and no hot air. The tube was not a stove at all, but it cooked. The company filed a
patent for cooking with microwaves on 8 October 1945, and the first oven, the 1947 Radarange, was a water-cooled
cabinet almost six feet tall that weighed 750 pounds and went to restaurants and ships. Twenty years later, in 1967, a
countertop home model sold for 495 dollars.
