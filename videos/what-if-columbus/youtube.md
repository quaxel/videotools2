## Title
Columbus Lied to His Own Crew. What If They Had Turned Back? ⛵ #Shorts

## Description
Columbus lied to his own crew about how far they'd sailed, and it still almost wasn't enough.

In September 1492 three small ships, the Santa María, the Pinta and the Niña, sailed west from the Canary Islands. Columbus's log survives only in a summary made by the friar Bartolomé de las Casas, and it says he kept two counts of distance: the true one, and a shorter one for the crew so they would not lose heart. On 10 October the men lost patience and complained of the length of the voyage; the log says he calmed them, and the later chronicler Oviedo says he promised to turn back if no land appeared within three days. The next day the sea sent clues: a stick that looked carved with an iron tool and a branch loaded with berries. At about two in the morning on 12 October, Rodrigo de Triana, a sailor on the Pinta, sighted land. Had they turned back, someone else would almost certainly have crossed soon: a Portuguese fleet under Pedro Álvares Cabral reached Brazil in 1500. This crossing started the Columbian exchange: potatoes, maize and cacao went to the Old World, while smallpox and other Old World diseases went to the Americas, where together with war and enslavement they killed an estimated 80 to 95 percent of the native population. And the reward for the first sighting, 10,000 maravedís a year from the crown, went to Columbus himself, who said he had seen a light first.

Would the voyage have been tried again within a few years? 👇
