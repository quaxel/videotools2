# Research: The Real Insect Trapped in Relay 70

## Mechanism

The Harvard Mark II (the Aiken Relay Calculator) was an electromechanical computer built at Harvard for the US Navy
out of electromagnetic relays: small switches in which current through a coil makes a magnet that pulls an armature,
and the armature pushes two springy contact points together so current can pass on to the next part of the
calculation. On 9 September 1947, while the machine was being tested, its operators traced an error to Panel F,
Relay number 70, and found a moth trapped between the relay's points: with the insect's body between them the two
contacts could not close properly, so the relay could not pass its signal. They taped the moth into the machine's
logbook at the 1545 (15:45) entry, "Relay #70 Panel F (moth) in relay.", and wrote under it "First actual case of
bug being found." The joke is the word "actual": engineers had called small faults "bugs" for decades (Thomas Edison
wrote of "Bugs" as "such little faults and difficulties" in an 1878 letter), and "debugging" already existed; this
time the bug was a real insect. Grace Hopper, who worked on the Mark II as a civilian research fellow at the Harvard
Computation Laboratory (she had left active Navy duty in 1946 and stayed in the reserve), told the story many times
and made it famous; the Smithsonian says the logbook was probably not hers, and who found the moth is not certain.
The page with the moth is in the collection of the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History (object
1994.0191.01), not on public display.

## Left out

- "Crashed", "locked the whole machine", "stopped dead": no source says the moth halted the Mark II; sources say an error
  was traced to it. Script and headline say "something went wrong" / an error, not that it stopped.
- The moth's size (5 cm / two inches / about an inch): sources disagree. It is drawn at about 2x for the phone
  (format_note).
- "Roasted", "shorted": one source (St Andrews says "shorted"); the NHHC caption says "trapped between points". Left out;
  the sparks in the hook are staging.
- Tweezers: no source says how it was pulled out. Not spoken; if shown, staging.
- William "Bill" Burke as one of the operators: single source (Wikipedia, blog comments). Not named.
- The relay count (13,000 / 17,000), the weight (25 tons) and the floor area (4,000 sq ft): single sources or in
  disagreement. Not spoken; the machine is shown as a room of relay racks.
- That Hopper found the moth herself (St Andrews, several blogs): contradicted by the Smithsonian's "probably not
  Hopper's". The script says "the operators" and that Hopper told the story.
- Dahlgren 1988 museum and the wrong 1945 date on its caption: a side story.
- The other logbook lines (0800 started, 1700 closed down, the Cosine Tape): shown only as unreadable scribbles.
- Company names of later machines Hopper worked on: not used.
