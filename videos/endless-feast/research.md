# Research: The King Who Banned the Music From Stopping

People get used to a pleasant experience while it lasts, so its enjoyment fades (hedonic adaptation), and they do not
expect this. Leif D. Nelson, Tom Meyvis and Jeff Galak (Journal of Consumer Research 36(2), 2009, "Enhancing the
Television-Viewing Experience through Commercial Interruptions") showed that commercial breaks interrupt this
adaptation and restore the intensity of enjoyment: across their studies people preferred to watch without
interruptions, yet the interrupted programmes were enjoyed more. The effect held regardless of the quality of the ads
and across different kinds of interruption, and it disappeared for people less prone to adaptation and for programmes
that do not produce adaptation. The frame of the video is a story: a Shah whose victory feast must never stop, and the
ring inscribed "This too shall pass", a fable that the phrase's history traces to medieval Persian Sufi poets.

## Claims

- Nelson, Meyvis and Galak, "Enhancing the Television-Viewing Experience through Commercial Interruptions", Journal of Consumer Research 36(2), 160-172, published in two thousand nine (2009) — https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-597030.html
- Enjoyment of a programme fades over time as viewers adapt to it, which they do not anticipate — https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-597030.html

## Left out

- The number of studies (six in the abstract): only one source states it, and the script does not need it.
- The poets' names (Attar, Sanai, Rumi): sources disagree on who told the ring fable first and when.
- The Solomon version of the ring: a later, separate tradition.
- Any claim that breaks make every pleasure better: the effect needs adaptation and vanishes for people who adapt little.
- The Shah, the feast, the blacksmith and the snapped lute string are story, not history.
