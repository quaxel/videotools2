# Research: The Dropped Flask That Saved Millions of Drivers

## Mechanism

In 1903 the French chemist and artist Édouard Bénédictus (born in Paris on 29 June 1878, died there in 1930; artist,
designer, composer and chemist) knocked a glass flask to the floor of his lab. It shattered, but the pieces did not
fly apart: the flask kept its shape. It had once held a solution of collodion (cellulose nitrate, a plastic made by
treating cotton with nitric and sulfuric acids, dissolved in alcohol and ether); the solvent had evaporated and left
a thin, clear plastic film on the inside of the glass, and the film held the cracked pieces together. The story goes
(the versions differ on who was hurt) that he later read about a car accident in which people were cut by broken
glass, remembered the flask and spent the night coating glass; the result was a sandwich of two sheets of glass with
a sheet of cellulose nitrate (celluloid) bonded between them. He patented it in 1909. Car makers did not take it up
at first (it was costly); its first wide use was in the eyepieces of gas masks in the First World War. Laminated
glass later became standard in car windscreens; since the late 1930s the layer in the middle has been polyvinyl
butyral (PVB), and when a windscreen breaks the fragments stick to that layer instead of flying out.

  story goes".
- "By morning" / "within a day": single-sourced; dropped.

- "Saved millions of drivers": no source counts lives saved; not spoken.
- The year cars made it standard (1919, 1920s, 1927 differ by source): left out.
