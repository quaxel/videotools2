# Research: What if Lincoln's guard had stayed at his door? (Ford's Theatre, 14 April 1865)

## Mechanism

On the night of 14 April 1865, five days after Lee's surrender at Appomattox, Abraham Lincoln watched the comedy *Our American Cousin* from the State Box at Ford's Theatre in Washington. The Metropolitan Police officer assigned to guard the entrance to the box, John Frederick Parker, was not at his post when it mattered: during the intermission he reportedly went to the saloon next door (the Star Saloon) with Lincoln's footman Charles Forbes and coachman Francis Burke, and whether he ever returned to the theatre is unknown. Three days earlier, on 11 April, Lincoln had told a crowd outside the White House that he would prefer the vote be given to "the very intelligent, and on those who serve our cause as soldiers" among Black men; according to an account traced through Herold's lawyer Frederick Stone to the reporter George Alfred Townsend (disputed by some historians), the actor John Wilkes Booth stood in that crowd. Shortly after ten, Booth climbed to the dress circle, showed Forbes a card, entered the passage behind the box and barred its outer door with a pine bar wedged against the wall. He timed his single shot to the big laugh that followed Harry Hawk's line "you sockdologizing old man-trap!" Lincoln died at 7:22 a.m. on 15 April. Andrew Johnson became president; he issued over 13,000 pardons to former Confederates and in 1866 vetoed the Freedmen's Bureau bill and the Civil Rights bill (Congress overrode the Civil Rights veto). Historians disagree on how far Lincoln's survival would have changed Reconstruction: some (Censer, Foner) stress how unlike Johnson he was, others stress that Congress and the clash with Johnson shaped the outcome. Parker was charged with neglect of duty on 1 May 1865; the complaint was dismissed on 2 June.

## Left out
- That Parker "got drunk and fell asleep" (Wikipedia's wording): not well sourced; the script keeps only "reportedly heads to the saloon".
- That Booth drank at the Star Saloon beside Parker: a popular story, not firmly sourced; Booth is not shown in the saloon.
- Booth's remark at the 11 April speech (a racist slur and "That is the last speech he will ever give"): the slur stays off screen and the remark is not quoted; the script says only that Booth was reportedly in the crowd.
- The music-stand origin of the bar: forum and secondary sources only; the script says "a wooden plank".
- The peephole bored in the box door: disputed among researchers; not shown.
- Parker's 1868 dismissal for sleeping on duty: true (Norton) but a side track.
- The exact number of pardons (13,000+; 12,652 by June 1866 in another count): sources differ on the total, so the script says "thousands".
- Rathbone's stabbing and Booth's leap to the stage: violent detail outside the story; not shown.
- Whether Booth would have been stopped had Parker stayed: no source can know; the script hedges with "Maybe".
