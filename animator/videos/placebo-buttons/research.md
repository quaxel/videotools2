# Research: Why the close door button often does nothing

## Mechanism

Under the 1990 Americans with Disabilities Act, US elevator doors must stay fully open for at least 3 seconds in
response to a call, and the door-signal timing (at least 5 seconds from arrival) also has to run out. The Access
Board's guide says user activation of door close cannot shorten either. Because the minimum time and the normal
hold time are often the same, many owners program the button so it has no effect in everyday use; the door-open
button still works. The close button is not removed because firefighters need it: in fire service mode (switched on
with a key) automatic closing is off and the doors close only while the firefighter holds door close. Crosswalks have
the same kind of placebo: in 2004 New York's transport department said more than 2,500 of its 3,250 walk buttons
did nothing, relics of the 1970s before computers timed the signals, and removing them would cost about $1 million.

## Claims

- Functional close-door buttons have been phased out since the Americans with Disabilities Act passed in 1990 (nineteen ninety), per the head of the National Elevator Industry; door-open buttons still work — https://www.cbsnews.com/boston/news/door-close-elevator-button-dont-work-placebo
- Doors must remain fully open at least 3 seconds (three seconds) in response to a call; user activation of door close cannot reduce that minimum open time or the door and signal timing — https://www.access-board.gov/files/ada/guides/elevators.pdf
- The door close button is required to operate the elevator in fire service mode, which turns off automatic door closing; outside the ADA minimum time the buttons can work, but in many elevators the minimum and normal hold times are the same — https://computer.rip/2023-03-13-the-door-close-button.html
- New York Times, February 27, 2004 (two thousand four): more than 2,500 (two thousand five hundred) of the 3,250 (three thousand two hundred fifty) walk buttons that still exist function essentially as mechanical placebos, city figures show — https://media.alexandriava.gov/docs-archives/localmotion/info/pushbuttonarticle.pdf
- Most of the buttons are relics of the 1970s (nineteen seventies), before computers began choreographing traffic signals — https://media.alexandriava.gov/docs-archives/localmotion/info/pushbuttonarticle.pdf
- At $300 or $400 per intersection it would cost about $1 million (a million dollars) to remove the disconnected mechanisms, so the city left them — https://media.alexandriava.gov/docs-archives/localmotion/info/pushbuttonarticle.pdf

Notes: the elevator industry disputes the word "placebo"; some buttons work after the minimum time, and pre-ADA
elevators may close at once. The script says "in many elevators", not "all". Office "dummy thermostats" were left
out: the share that are fake is disputed (2 % vs 90 %).
