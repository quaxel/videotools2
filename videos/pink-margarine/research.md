# Research: Why one state demanded pink margarine

## Mechanism

New Hampshire's Public Statutes of 1891 (ch. 127, §§ 19-20) let anyone sell a butter substitute, provided the
package was marked "Oleomargarine" in plain letters at least half an inch tall and, for a butter substitute,
"unless it is of a pink color". Breaking the rule carried a fine of up to $100, up to 60 days in prison, or both.
A dealer's agent in Manchester sold a ten-pound package of butter-coloured margarine (dyed with the colouring often
used for butter), was convicted and fined $100. In Collins v. New Hampshire (171 U.S. 30, decided May 23, 1898)
the U.S. Supreme Court reversed: pink is not margarine's natural colour, the law forced the seller to add a
foreign substance that made the product unsalable, and a permission to sell tied to a condition that prevents any
sale "amounts in law to a prohibition". The court added that if pink were allowed, a legislature could equally
demand blue, red or black, or an offensive smell. Justices Harlan and Gray dissented.
