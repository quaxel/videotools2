# Research — thames-tide

In the summer of 1858 London's sewers still emptied straight into the Thames. At Westminster the Thames is a tidal
river: what the sewers poured in did not leave for the sea but was carried down on the ebb and back up on the flood.
In the Commons on 9 July 1858 General Thompson said the sewage poured in at low water "could do nothing but be
bandied up and down under the noses of the citizens"; in December 1857 the First Commissioner, Sir Benjamin Hall, had
rejected an outfall just outside London because "the sewage would have flowed back with the tide into the
metropolitan area". During the heat (30 degrees for weeks, London Museum) members could not stay in the committee
rooms and library (Hansard, 11 June 1858) and peers talked of meeting somewhere away from the river (Lords, 25 June).
The government's immediate step was chemical: "100 tons of lime or more with other chemicals" poured into the river
at the mouths of the sewers (Lords, 28 June 1858). It could not stop the tide bringing the filth back. The answer
passed in July 1858 was the Metropolitan Board of Works' system of intercepting sewers (Chancellor of the Exchequer,
15 July 1858), with outfalls far downstream at Barking Creek and Crossness, "a point so low that none of it should
flow back again" (Sir John Shelley). Joseph Bazalgette, the Board's engineer, built 82 miles of brick intercepting
sewers flowing east; at Crossness the sewage was pumped into a reservoir holding 27 million gallons, and the sluices
were opened just before high tide so it flowed out on the ebb, toward the sea. The bill became law in 18 days.

Limits: no opened source gives a tidal period or a speed at Westminster, so the script says the tide turns without a
number. The returning hat is story, not a claim. Curtains soaked in chloride of lime are documented for Londoners in
general (London Museum), not specifically for Parliament: the video shows a soaked curtain only as decoration and says
nothing about it. The MP is a composite, unnamed character.
