## Title
What If Fleming Had Cleaned Up His Lab? The Messy Bench That Found Penicillin 🧫 #Shorts

## Description
Before penicillin, an infected scratch could kill you. Then one scientist left a messy bench and went on holiday.

In August 1928 Alexander Fleming, at St Mary's Hospital in London, went on his summer holiday and left dishes of Staphylococcus, the germ behind boils and blood poisoning, on his lab bench instead of in the warm incubator. Back on 3 September he found a blue-green mould on one dish, and all around it the germs were dead. "That's funny," he said. The mould was making a substance that kills bacteria; he called it penicillin. Fleming could not turn it into a usable medicine. In 1940 Howard Florey's team at Oxford, with Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley, finally did, in tiny amounts. American factories then scaled it up to 2.3 million doses in time for D-Day. Ronald Hare later argued that if the dishes had gone into the incubator, the germs would have grown first and the mould would have done nothing. By some estimates penicillin and the antibiotics that followed have saved hundreds of millions of lives.

Would you have tidied up? 👇
