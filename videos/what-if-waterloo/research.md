# Research: What if Napoleon's marshal had marched to the guns? (Waterloo, 18 June 1815)

## Mechanism

Two days before Waterloo, Napoleon beat Blücher's Prussians at Ligny but did not destroy them. On 17 June he split his army: about 33,000 men under Marshal Grouchy were to pursue the Prussians and keep them away from Wellington, while Napoleon took the rest against Wellington at Mont-Saint-Jean. Rain on 17 June and through the night left the ground soaked, and Napoleon did not open the battle until about 11:30 on 18 June. Grouchy set off late and slowly, and lost track of the Prussians, who were not retreating east but marching west to join Wellington. At about 11:30, during a breakfast halt at a notary's house in Sart-lez-Walhain (where, the story goes, he was eating strawberries), Grouchy and his staff heard the cannonade. General Gérard urged him to march to the sound of the guns; Grouchy refused, citing Napoleon's orders to follow the Prussians, and pushed on to Wavre. There he fought only the Prussian rear guard (Thielmann's III Corps) while Blücher's other three corps marched to Waterloo. From late afternoon Bülow's IV Corps attacked the French right flank at Plancenoit, pinning Napoleon's reserves. When the Imperial Guard's final attack failed around 7:30 p.m., the French army broke and fled. The counterfactual is weak: historians argue Grouchy probably could not have stopped the Prussian junction in time, and that even a victory on 18 June would have left Napoleon facing the much larger Austrian and Russian armies already heading for France.

## Claims

- The Battle of Waterloo was fought on Sunday 18 June 1815 (June eighteenth, eighteen fifteen) near Waterloo in present-day Belgium; it ended Napoleon's Hundred Days return from exile — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo
- After eleven months on Elba, Napoleon returned to France in March 1815 and restored his empire, raising a new army; the battle of 18 June (June eighteenth, eighteen fifteen) ended it — https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/waterloo
- On 16 June 1815, two days earlier (two days before the eighteenth), Napoleon "inflicted a bruising defeat on Blücher at Ligny" — https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/waterloo
- Napoleon attacked the Prussians first, "mauling Blücher's army at Ligny on 16th June" (two days before Waterloo); the Prussians retreated but were not destroyed — https://www.history.co.uk/history-of-the-battle-of-waterloo/waterloo
- Napoleon assigned 33,000 men (over thirty thousand) under Marshal Emmanuel de Grouchy to drive Blücher out of Belgium; he did not release the pursuit until 11:00 a.m. on 17 June, a day earlier than the battle — https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/waterloo
- On the morning of 17 June (a day earlier), Napoleon gave Grouchy a detachment of about 33,765 men (over thirty thousand; 38,870 before the losses of 16 June) and 96 guns, III and IV Corps plus cavalry, with orders to "pursue the Prussians" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign:_Ligny_through_Wavre_to_Waterloo
- Grouchy was "given 33,000 men" (over thirty thousand) to push the Prussians away from Wellington; he lost touch with them on 17 June, partly because Napoleon didn't set him going until late in the morning — https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_grouchy.html
- Sources vary slightly on the figure: the Battle of Wavre article gives 33,000 men and 80 guns, its infobox 32,000–33,000, and 33,765 at Wavre (all over thirty thousand) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wavre
- Heavy rain the night before left the ground wet; Drouot advised delaying the battle so it could dry, and Napoleon agreed, which cost hours — https://frenchempire.net/battles/waterloo
- On 18 June "mud prevented Napoleon from launching his attack until 11:30 a.m." (after the rainy day of 17 June) — https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/waterloo
- The battle began at around 11:30; this page notes it "has often been said" Napoleon delayed to let the ground dry but argues the delay was more about getting troops into position (the rain's importance is debated) — https://www.thenapoleonicwars.net/battle-of-waterloo
- A Brown University timeline puts the first shots at 11:20 a.m. (so 11:00–11:30 depending on source) — https://library.brown.edu/cds/askb/waterloo/battle.html
- Gérard reached Sart-lez-Walhain at about 11:00 on 18 June and found Grouchy eating breakfast at the house of a notary (spelled "Hollaëbt" here); at about 11:30 Colonel Lorière heard a distant, violent cannonade from Waterloo and the notary pointed out its direction toward the Forest of Soignies and Mont-Saint-Jean — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign:_Ligny_through_Wavre_to_Waterloo
- The strawberries (a story/tradition): one account says Grouchy stopped at a notary's house in Walhain, Gérard was directed to "Monsieur Hollert's house", and Grouchy was inside "eating strawberries from a basket on the table" when gunfire was heard from the west — https://adventuresinhistoryland.com/2015/07/07/grouchy-the-invincible/
- The strawberries (a story/tradition): Belgian newspaper L'Avenir says Grouchy left too late, "faisant une trop longue halte chez un notaire de Walhain pour savourer des fraises" (a long halt at a Walhain notary's to savour strawberries) — https://www.lavenir.net/cnt/dmf20150602_00658631
- Grouchy's corps commanders, especially Gérard (IV Corps), suggested they "march to the sound of the guns"; Grouchy had written and verbal orders from Napoleon to march on Wavre and engage the Prussians there, and declined — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wavre
- Gérard volunteered IV Corps to march toward the guns; Grouchy refused, saying his duty was to carry out Napoleon's instructions and that splitting off toward the Forest of Soignies would break his orders — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign:_Ligny_through_Wavre_to_Waterloo
- Gérard and Vandamme both urged Grouchy to march west immediately toward the sound of the artillery at Waterloo — https://www.frenchempire.net/battles/wavre/
- One retelling gives Gérard's words as "It is your duty to march towards the cannon!" and Grouchy's reply as his duty "is to execute the emperor's orders, which direct me to follow the Prussians" (wording varies by source) — https://adventuresinhistoryland.com/2015/07/07/grouchy-the-invincible/
- The Napoleon Foundation's book notice (Dawson, Napoleon and Grouchy) asks whether Gérard really told Grouchy to march to the sound of the guns — the exact exchange is disputed — https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/napoleon-grouchy-last-great-waterloo-mystery-unravelled
- Wavre, where Grouchy fought, was "only ten miles away" from Waterloo; his men heard Napoleon's Grand Battery sounding to the west — https://www.warhistoryonline.com/napoleon/battle-wavre-ten-miles-away-waterloo-another-battle-may-sealed-napoleons-fate-m.html
- At Wavre the Prussian rear guard was Thielmann's III Corps (about 15,200–17,000 men) against Grouchy's 33,765; Blücher sent three of his four corps (I, II and IV) to Waterloo — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wavre
- At Wavre Grouchy defeated the Prussian rear guard but did not realise until too late that he was dealing only with Thielmann's III Corps — https://historynet.com/why-did-napoleon-and-grouchy-behave-as-they-did-during-the-battle-of-waterloo/
- Most of the Prussians slipped away to intervene decisively at Waterloo in the late afternoon while a rear guard kept Grouchy's wing occupied — https://www.frenchempire.net/battles/wavre/
- By 4:30 p.m. (late afternoon) the Prussians were advancing on Plancenoit on the French right; fighting there lasted until 8 p.m. — https://www.thenapoleonicwars.net/battle-of-waterloo
- By 5 p.m. the Prussians were in street fighting with the French at Plancenoit on Napoleon's right flank — https://www.history.co.uk/history-of-the-battle-of-waterloo/waterloo
- At 7:30 p.m. Napoleon sent eight battalions of the Guard into a final assault, which was repulsed; the French army began fleeing the battlefield around 8:30 p.m. — https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/waterloo
- At 7 p.m. Napoleon sent the Imperial Guard against the Allied centre; "As the 'invincible' Imperial Guard began to retreat, the entire French army lost heart" — https://www.history.co.uk/history-of-the-battle-of-waterloo/waterloo
- Wellington to Thomas Creevey in Brussels the morning after the battle (19 June 1815): "It has been a damned nice thing – the nearest run thing you ever saw in your life" (as quoted in Bryant, The Great Duke) — https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/period-glossary/close
- The battle article gives the wording as "the nearest-run thing you ever saw in your life" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo
- Historians on whether marching would have helped: Siborne argues Grouchy should have started earlier and gone via Saint-Lambert, but concludes he "could have effected nothing" beyond delaying the Prussian–Anglo-allied junction, which could not have been prevented — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign:_Ligny_through_Wavre_to_Waterloo
- Grouchy was "miles behind the Prussians and too far to the east" for his orders to have much effect — https://grognard.com/spotlight/waterloo-near-run-thing.html
- Grouchy's choice to obey orders "instead of marching to the sound of the guns" was "understandable, although also a mistake" — https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_grouchy.html
- The Seventh Coalition (Britain, Prussia, Austria, Russia) planned invasion forces of almost 1 million men against France — https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/waterloo
- Schwarzenberg's Austrian Army of the Upper Rhine, aimed at Paris, crossed the Rhine from 19 June (Bavarian IV Corps at Mannheim) to 25 June; Barclay de Tolly's Russian army followed, its advance guard occupying Saarbrücken on 24 June and the main body crossing at Mannheim on 25 June — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_campaigns_of_1815
- Barclay de Tolly again served as commander-in-chief of the Russian army during the Hundred Days — https://www.napoleon.org/en/reading_room/biographies/files/barclay_tolly.asp
- "To meet one's Waterloo" means to suffer a decisive, crushing defeat, alluding to Napoleon's final defeat at Waterloo on 18 June 1815; early uses: "a Waterloo defeat" (Nashville Whig, 1824), "meet with a Waterloo" (The Courier, London, 1832), "meet their Waterloo" (Public Ledger, Philadelphia, 1838) — https://wordhistories.net/2024/03/19/meet-ones-waterloo/
- Blücher (born 16 December 1742, so 72 at Waterloo) was called "Marschall Vorwärts" (Marshal Forward); he is quoted calling "Forwards!" to his men and saying "I have given my promise to Wellington, and you surely don't want me to break it?" (quoted via Cornwell) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gebhard_Leberecht_von_Bl%C3%BCcher
- The Napoleon Foundation explains Blücher's nickname « Vorwärts » (« en avant », Forward) — https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/dossiers-thematiques/blucher-et-la-campagne-de-waterloo/

## Left out

- The notary's name: spelled "Hollert" in one retelling and "Hollaëbt" in another, and one source does not make clear the house was his; an 1893 letter even puts Grouchy at Château Marette in Walhain rather than Sart-lez-Walhain. The script just says "at breakfast", which avoids the name.
- Exact hour of the strawberries and of the first cannon sound: 11:00 (Gérard arrives), "about 11:30" (cannonade) or "just after 11:15", depending on the account. The script gives no time.
- Gérard's exact words: sources paraphrase ("march to the sound of the guns", "It is your duty to march towards the cannon!"), and Dawson questions whether he said it at all. The script's line is a dramatisation, not a quote.
- Exact start time: 11:00, 11:20, 11:30, or "1:30 p.m." for Ney's main attack; the script avoids a clock time. Whether the rain really caused the delay is also debated (troop positions may matter more).
- Exact size of Grouchy's force: 32,000, 33,000, 33,765 (and 25,000+ infantry on one road). The script's "over thirty thousand" fits all of them.
- Prussian arrival time: 4:30 p.m. near Plancenoit, 5 p.m. street fighting, first attack on the village at 6 p.m.; Brown says Prussians closed in from 1:15–2:15 p.m. The script's "late afternoon" covers this.
- Guard attack time: 7:00 or 7:30 p.m. The "La Garde recule!" cry is famous but not confirmed by any page opened, so it is not used.
- Napoleon's alleged remark that "not a man would have escaped" if Grouchy had come instead of the Prussians: only in a reader comment, unsourced.
- Wellington's "Give me night or give me Blücher": popular but disputed, and not in the script.
- The date of the Creevey quote: given as 18 June on some pages and the morning after (19 June) on others; the script names no date.
- Blücher's age: some popular sources say 74; from his birth date he was 72. Flavour only, so it is not spoken.
- Uniform colours (French blue, British red, Prussian dark blue, Austrian white, Russian dark green): no page opened confirmed them, so there is no claim line. Treat them as standard art direction, not sourced fact.
- Casualty numbers: left out entirely. Figures differ between sources and the Short avoids grim detail.
- Distance from Sart-lez-Walhain to Waterloo (about 23 km is commonly given): only seen in a search snippet, not on an opened page; the script's "miles away" rests on the "ten miles" Wavre–Waterloo source.
