# Research: Why 'You Deserve It' Picks the Wrong Box

The medieval Gesta Romanorum tells of a test with three vessels. The gold one is set with precious stones outside and
carries the words "Whoso chooseth me, shall find that he deserveth"; inside are dead men's bones. The silver one says
"Whoso chooseth me, shall find that his nature desireth" and holds earth and worms. The plain lead one says "Whoso
chooseth me, shall find that God hath disposed for him" and holds fine gold and precious stones. In the tale the king's
daughter chooses, prays, rejects gold and silver and takes lead. The trap in the gold vessel is that it speaks to the
chooser's sense of what they have earned. Modern consumer research names that sense: Lisa A. Cavanaugh (Journal of
Marketing Research, 2014) found that perceived deservingness, not feelings or mood, most accurately predicts whether
people indulge (treat themselves): consumers who feel less deserving restrict indulgence, consumers who feel deserving
indulge. The video keeps the two apart: the tale supplies the wrong box, the study supplies only the link between
"I deserve it" and treating yourself. It does not claim the study shows deserving people choose wrongly.

## Claims

- The Gesta Romanorum is a collection of medieval tales; "The Three Caskets" is one of them — https://www.kellscraft.com/MediaevalTales/MediaevalTales03.html
- The gold vessel's inscription: "Whoso chooseth me, shall find that he deserveth"; it is set with precious stones outside and full of dead men's bones inside — https://www.kellscraft.com/MediaevalTales/MediaevalTales03.html
- The silver vessel's inscription: "Whoso chooseth me, shall find that his nature desireth"; it holds earth and worms — https://www.kellscraft.com/MediaevalTales/MediaevalTales03.html
- The lead vessel's inscription: "Whoso chooseth me, shall find that God hath disposed for him"; inside it are fine gold and precious stones — https://www.kellscraft.com/MediaevalTales/MediaevalTales03.html
- In the tale a young woman (the king's daughter) makes the choice and takes the lead vessel — https://www.kellscraft.com/MediaevalTales/MediaevalTales03.html
- Lisa A. Cavanaugh, "Because I (Don't) Deserve It: How Relationship Reminders and Deservingness Influence Consumer Indulgence", Journal of Marketing Research 51(2), 218-232, published in twenty fourteen (2014) — https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmr.12.0133
- Perceived deservingness predicts consumers' propensity to indulge; "it is the perceptions of deservingness but not feelings or mood that most accurately predict whether indulgence occurs" — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/03/140312103502.htm
- Consumers who feel less deserving restrict indulgent consumption (spend less, pick lower-end brands) — https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmr.12.0133

## Left out

- The number of studies: the paper's abstract says five studies, the press release says seven experiments. Sources disagree, so no number.
- The study's own manipulation (reminders of relationships people lack, Valentine's cards): true but a second idea; the video needs only "deserving predicts treating yourself, mood does not".
- Any claim that feeling deserving makes people choose badly, or that "you deserve it" slogans work: the study does not test that. The shelf box at the end is shown, not claimed.
- The Merchant of Venice's version of the caskets (Portia, the suitors): well known, but a different text with different inscriptions.
- The tale's frame (the emperor's son, the whale, the princess's prayer): kept off the script; the knight is a fictional chooser, the woman stands in for the princess.
- Morley's 1884 edition details: the kellscraft page does not print the edition, so the video names only the Gesta Romanorum.
