# Research: What if the dinosaur-killing asteroid had hit somewhere else?

About sixty-six million years ago an asteroid roughly ten kilometres (six miles) or more across struck a shallow tropical
sea on what is now the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico (the Chicxulub crater). The target rock there was a thick marine
sequence rich in sulfur-bearing evaporites (gypsum, anhydrite) and organic matter / hydrocarbons. The blast threw dust
and vaporised rock into the stratosphere; the sulfur became sulfate aerosols, and in Kaiho & Oshima's reading the
burning hydrocarbons added soot. Sunlight dimmed, the planet cooled, photosynthesis collapsed and food chains failed
from the bottom up; about three quarters (75 %) of plant and animal species vanished, including all non-avian
dinosaurs (birds, the avian dinosaurs, survived). In two thousand seventeen Kunio Kaiho (Tohoku University) and Naga
Oshima (Meteorological Research Institute), two Japanese scientists, mapped hydrocarbon-rich sedimentary rock worldwide
and ran a climate model: only hydrocarbon-rich areas covering about thirteen percent of Earth's surface would have
made enough soot for the extreme cooling; on the other eighty-seven percent of the surface the cooling in their model
is far milder and, they argue, the dinosaurs could have survived. Other scientists (e.g. Sean Gulick, UT Austin)
question the study's assumptions and argue the sulfur released from the crater rock was enough to cause the cooling.
The small mammals that came through were tiny: the largest right after the event were no bigger than rats; with the
giants gone, mammals grew and spread.

## Claims

- Sixty-six million years ago (about 66 million years ago) an asteroid hit hydrocarbon- and sulfur-rich sedimentary rocks in what is now Mexico — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5680197/
- The impact heated those rocks, forming stratospheric soot and sulfate aerosols and causing extreme global cooling (Kaiho & Oshima 2017, Scientific Reports 7:14855) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5680197/
- Only hydrocarbon-rich areas occupying approximately 13% (about thirteen percent) of Earth's surface would have produced the mass extinction — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5680197/
- 87 percent (the other eighty-seven percent) of the planet's surface was less dense in hydrocarbons; an impact there gives far milder cooling in their model, and "it is likely the dinosaurs would have survived" — https://phys.org/news/2017-11-analysis-chicxulub-asteroid-struck-vulnerable.html
- The study (two thousand seventeen, i.e. 2017) is by two Japanese scientists, Kunio Kaiho of Tohoku University and Naga Oshima of the Meteorological Research Institute; they mapped hydrocarbon amounts worldwide and simulated the soot — https://phys.org/news/2017-11-analysis-chicxulub-asteroid-struck-vulnerable.html
- The impactor was about ten kilometres (six miles) in diameter — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicxulub_impactor
- The impactor is believed to have been about 10–15 km in diameter (six miles wide or more) — https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-science/chicxulub-asteroid-killed-dinosaurs
- It struck the tropical, shallow seas of the sulfur-rich Yucatán Peninsula (Mexico); a nearby oil field may have burned, adding soot — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicxulub_impactor
- The extinction killed about 75% (about three quarters) of plant and animal species, including all non-avian dinosaurs (the giant dinosaurs; birds survived) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicxulub_impactor
- The loss of 75% of all plant and animal species around 66 million years ago — https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-science/chicxulub-asteroid-killed-dinosaurs
- Other scientists question the study's assumptions (Sean Gulick, UT Austin) — https://www.jhunewsletter.com/article/2017/11/dinosaurs-could-have-survived-the-asteroid-hit
- Gulick's team argues the impact released enough sulfate aerosols from the crater's sulfur-rich rock to cause global cooling and darkening (the dust and sulfur alone were enough) — https://ig.utexas.edu/news/2019/rocks-at-asteroid-impact-site-record-first-day-of-dinosaur-extinction/
- The largest mammals just after the event were no bigger than rats (Corral Bluffs, Colorado) — https://www.upi.com/Science_News/2019/10/28/Colorado-site-shows-how-mammals-thrived-after-dinosaurs-were-wiped-out/7701572275942/
- With the dinosaurs gone, mammals grew: within about 100,000 years raccoon-sized mammals were back, later much bigger — https://www.upi.com/Science_News/2019/10/28/Colorado-site-shows-how-mammals-thrived-after-dinosaurs-were-wiped-out/7701572275942/

## Left out

- Kaiho & Oshima's own impactor size, "approximately 9 km": the approved script says "six miles wide or more", which
  matches the common 10 km (six miles) and the 10–15 km range; do not "correct" it to 9 km.
- "Too cold for about three years" (phys.org): one source, and no degree figure; the script says only "went cold".
- Gulick's 325 billion tonnes of sulfur and the Krakatoa comparison: sources disagree on the comparison.
- The 13 % read as a "13 % chance" of extinction: some outlets frame it so; we say only what share of the surface.
- Exact sizes of the surviving mammals (hamster vs rat, 0.5 kg): we keep "most no bigger than a rat".
- Deccan Traps volcanism as a co-cause: a real debate, but a second mechanism would crowd the 60 s.
