# Research: Why supermarkets play slow music

## Mechanism

Background music sets the pace of a store. In Milliman's 1982 supermarket field study, slow-tempo music made
in-store traffic move more slowly than fast-tempo music, and sales volume on slow-music days was 38.2% higher
than on fast-music days (it was not significantly different from no music at all, so the effect is mostly that
fast music hurries people out). Music also primes what people pick: in a Leicester wine-shop experiment, French
accordion music and German oompah music were played on alternate days; French wine took 77% of sales on French
days and German wine 73% on German days, while only 1 of 44 questioned customers mentioned the music.

## Claims

- Milliman (1982), supermarket field study: 38.2% (thirty-eight percent) higher sales volume when slow tempo music was played rather than fast tempo music; no significant difference between no music and slow music (nineteen eighty-two) — https://pipedown.org.uk/more-suspect-research-about-piped-musics-benefits
- Milliman (1982): shoppers move clearly slower when a low tempo (72 BPM) is played; tempos of 92 BPM or more speed them up (fast songs hurried people along) — https://www.retaildesignlab.be/en/speed-movement-consumer
- Wine shop experiment (North, Hargreaves and McKendrick): French accordion music and German oompah music on alternate days; on French music days 77% (seventy-seven percent) of the wine sold was French, on German music days 73% (seventy-three percent) was German — https://mindhacks.com/2006/02/06/music-wine-and-will/
- Only 1 out of 44 (forty-four) customers who answered questions at the checkout spontaneously mentioned the music — https://mindhacks.com/2006/02/06/music-wine-and-will/

Notes: Milliman's 38.2% compares slow with fast music, not with silence; the script says "higher than on fast days".
Later studies (Knoeferle et al. 2012) found the tempo effect depends on the music's mode, so it is not a universal law.
The wine study ran in one UK store over two weeks.
