# Research: Why airlines sell more tickets than seats

## Mechanism

A seat is a perishable product: once the plane takes off, an empty seat can never be sold again. Some
booked passengers never show up, especially on flexible fares, and others cancel too late to resell. Airline
revenue-management systems know the historical no-show share for each route, so they sell extra tickets to
fill the seats the no-shows would leave empty. When more passengers show up than expected, the airline first
asks for volunteers in exchange for an offer; if not enough volunteer, passengers are bumped involuntarily
and, in the US, owed compensation set by DOT rule. Denied boarding is rare overall.

## Claims

- An aircraft seat is perishable: once the flight takes off it can never be sold again — https://www.iata.org/contentassets/2e46aace261040b9a47fb7b9da18efc9/overbooking.pdf
- Revenue management systems know the historical percentage of no-show passengers for any given route — https://www.iata.org/contentassets/2e46aace261040b9a47fb7b9da18efc9/overbooking.pdf
- Airlines first call for volunteers in exchange for an agreed offer before bumping anyone — https://www.iata.org/contentassets/2e46aace261040b9a47fb7b9da18efc9/overbooking.pdf
- About 0.09% of US passengers were denied boarding in 2016 (voluntary and involuntary), roughly 9 in 10,000 (nine in ten thousand, twenty sixteen) — https://www.iata.org/contentassets/2e46aace261040b9a47fb7b9da18efc9/overbooking.pdf
- No-show rates are said to range from 5 to 15 percent (five to fifteen percent) depending on season and route; with a ten percent expected no-show rate an airline may overbook by 5–10 percent; worked example: a 160-seat (hundred and sixty) Boeing 737-800 overbooked by 16 (sixteen) tickets — https://www.altexsoft.com/blog/overbooking-airlines-bumping/
- US domestic involuntary bump, arriving two hours or more late: 400% (up to four times) of the one-way fare, capped at $2,150; 1–2 hours late: 200%, capped at $1,075 — https://ecfr.io/Title-14/Section-250.5 (mirror of 14 CFR 250.5; ecfr.gov blocked automated access)

Notes: the no-show range is an industry estimate from a secondary source, not an official statistic. The 0.09% figure is from 2016.
