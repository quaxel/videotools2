## 1. What if Petrov had reported the launch?

1. In nineteen eighty-three, a computer told one Soviet officer that American missiles were coming. He decided the computer was wrong.
2. September twenty-sixth, just after midnight. A secret bunker outside Moscow.
3. Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov is on watch. Weeks earlier, Soviet jets shot down a Korean airliner. Moscow fears an American first strike.
4. Then the siren. One American missile, launched. Then another. Then five.
5. *(officer)* "Comrade Petrov! What do we report?"
6. His job: pass it up the chain. Moscow would have minutes to decide.
7. You see, a real first strike wouldn't be five missiles. It would be hundreds.
8. And ground radar had nothing to confirm yet.
9. *(Petrov)* "False alarm. Report a system fault."
10. Then he waits. Minutes pass.
11. *(officer)* "Still nothing, sir."
12. The satellite had mistaken sunlight glinting off high clouds for rocket fire.
13. Now, what if he'd reported an attack? His warning would have climbed to the Kremlin.
14. Would they have fired? Some say other checks would have stopped it. Nobody wants to find out.
15. His reward? No medal back then. A reprimand, he said, over paperwork. The story stayed secret for fifteen years.
16. Petrov later called it fifty-fifty. He bet the computer was wrong. And he was right.

## 2. What if the Archduke's driver had known the route?

1. A driver took one wrong turn, and stopped right in front of an assassin.
2. Sarajevo, June twenty-eighth, nineteen fourteen. Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne, rides in an open car.
3. That morning, a young man throws a bomb.
4. *(crowd)* "Bomb!"
5. It bounces off and blows up under the next car.
6. The plot seems dead. Another plotter, nineteen-year-old Gavrilo Princip, drifts to a street corner.
7. The Archduke changes plans. He'll visit the wounded in hospital.
8. But nobody tells the drivers.
9. The car in front turns down the old route. His driver follows.
10. *(governor)* "Wrong way! Stop!"
11. The driver brakes to reverse. It stops, a few steps from Princip.
12. Two shots. The Archduke and his wife Sophie are killed.
13. You see, Austria blamed Serbia, and declared war one month later. Russia backed Serbia. Germany backed Austria. France backed Russia.
14. Within a week, most of Europe was at war. About twenty million would die. Four empires would fall.
15. Now, what if the driver had known? The car speeds past, and Princip probably never gets his shot.
16. Would war have come anyway? Many historians think so. But not that day, and not from that spark.
17. And that spark came down to one wrong turn.

## 3. What if Fleming had cleaned up his lab?

1. Before penicillin, an infected scratch could kill you. Then one scientist left a messy bench and went on holiday.
2. London, nineteen twenty-eight. Alexander Fleming grows the germs that cause boils and blood poisoning.
3. That August he goes on holiday, leaving his germ dishes on the bench, not in the warm incubator.
4. September third, he's back.
5. *(Fleming)* "That's funny."
6. A blue-green mould has landed on one dish. And all around it, the germs are dead.
7. You see, the mould was making a chemical that kills bacteria. He called it penicillin.
8. But Fleming couldn't turn it into a usable medicine. Twelve years later, a team at Oxford finally did, in tiny amounts.
9. *(Oxford scientist)* "It works!"
10. Then American factories scaled it up. By D-Day, there were over two million doses.
11. Now, what if he'd tidied them into the incubator? One expert argued the germs would have grown first, and the mould would have done nothing. Maybe someone else finds it, but not in time for the war.
12. By some estimates, penicillin and the antibiotics it inspired have saved hundreds of millions of lives.
13. Maybe someone in your family tree. All thanks to one messy bench.

## 4. What if the asteroid had hit somewhere else?

1. The asteroid that killed the giant dinosaurs may have hit one of the unluckiest spots on Earth.
2. Sixty-six million years ago, a rock six miles wide or more slams into what is now Mexico.
3. *(dinosaur)* "Why is the sky so bright?"
4. It lands in a shallow sea, on rock packed with sulfur and buried oil.
5. You see, the blast flung dust and sulfur high into the sky. Some scientists add soot from burning oil. Sunlight dimmed. The planet went cold.
6. Plants died. Then the plant eaters. Then the meat eaters.
7. About three quarters of all species vanished.
8. In two thousand seventeen, two Japanese scientists mapped that oily rock worldwide.
9. They found it covers only about thirteen percent of Earth's surface.
10. Anywhere in the other eighty-seven percent, their model says, the cooling is far milder.
11. The giant dinosaurs might have made it through. Other scientists say the dust and sulfur alone were enough. Nobody can rerun the test.
12. Our ancestors? Small furry animals, most no bigger than a rat.
13. *(little mammal)* "Is it safe yet?"
14. With the giants gone, mammals got the planet. They grew. They spread.
15. Today, one of their descendants is watching this video.
16. Unlucky spot for the dinosaurs. Lucky for you.

## 5. What if Columbus had turned back?

1. Columbus lied to his own crew about how far they'd sailed. It still almost wasn't enough.
2. September, fourteen ninety-two. Three small ships sail west from the Canary Islands.
3. You see, Columbus keeps two counts of distance: a true one, and a shorter one for the crew, so they won't panic.
4. Weeks pass. No land.
5. October tenth. The crew has had enough.
6. *(sailor)* "Admiral, turn back!"
7. His log, known only from a friar's summary, says he calmed them. Some later writers say he made a deal: three more days.
8. Next day, the sea sends clues. A carved stick. A branch with berries.
9. *(sailor)* "Look! Someone carved this!"
10. Two in the morning, October twelfth, a lookout shouts.
11. *(lookout)* "Land! Land!"
12. Now, what if they had turned back? Someone else would almost certainly have crossed. Portugal reached Brazil in fifteen hundred.
13. But this crossing started the great swap. Potatoes and chocolate went east. Smallpox went west, and with war and slavery, killed most of the Americas' native people.
14. A lie, one tense night, and a few twigs. That's how close they came to turning around. And the lookout's reward? Columbus said he'd seen a light first, and kept it.

## 6. What if Lincoln's guard had stayed at his door?

1. The night Abraham Lincoln was shot, the policeman guarding his door wasn't there.
2. Washington, April fourteenth, eighteen sixty-five. The war is all but over.
3. Lincoln is at Ford's Theatre, watching a comedy from a private box.
4. His guard: police officer John Parker.
5. At intermission, Parker reportedly heads to the saloon next door.
6. *(barman)* "What'll it be, officer?"
7. Nobody knows if he came back. He wasn't at the door.
8. Three days earlier, Lincoln had backed voting rights for some Black men. Famous actor John Wilkes Booth was reportedly in the crowd.
9. Just after ten, Booth climbs the stairs.
10. He shows the footman a card, slips into the passage, and bars the door with a wooden plank.
11. Then he waits for the biggest laugh.
12. *(actor on stage)* "You sockdologizing old man-trap!"
13. One shot, hidden in the laughter. Lincoln dies the next morning.
14. Now, what if Parker had stayed? Maybe Booth is turned away. Maybe he tries another night.
15. Instead, Andrew Johnson took over. He pardoned thousands of Confederates and vetoed bills protecting freed slaves.
16. Many historians think Lincoln would have protected those rights better. Others say Congress mattered more.
17. And Parker? Charged with neglect of duty. The charge was dropped.
18. One empty chair, and a country took a different road.

## 7. What if Napoleon's marshal had marched to the guns?

1. Napoleon's best chance at Waterloo may have been sitting at breakfast, eating strawberries.
2. Belgium, June eighteenth, eighteen fifteen. Napoleon, back from exile, has a new army.
3. In front of him, Wellington. Somewhere east, the Prussians, beaten two days earlier.
4. A day earlier, Napoleon sent Marshal Grouchy and over thirty thousand men after the Prussians, to keep them away.
5. It rains all night. Napoleon waits for the mud to dry, giving the Prussians time.
6. Miles away, Grouchy is at breakfast. Strawberries, the story goes.
7. Then a low rumble, from the west. Cannons.
8. *(General Gérard)* "Marshal, we must march to the guns!"
9. *(Grouchy)* "My orders are to follow the Prussians."
10. So he keeps chasing, into a rear guard at Wavre. Most Prussians have already slipped away toward Waterloo.
11. *(Blücher)* "Forward! Faster!"
12. By late afternoon, they crash into Napoleon's side. His last attack breaks. His army runs.
13. Wellington called it the nearest run thing you ever saw.
14. Now, what if Grouchy had turned around? Some historians say he'd have arrived too late anyway.
15. Even winning that day probably wouldn't have saved Napoleon. Austrian and Russian armies were already marching on France.
16. Most historians think he'd still have lost, just somewhere else.
17. So the strawberries probably didn't change the war. Just which village gave us "meet your Waterloo."
