# Research: How the Mongol Yam relay carried messages 200 miles a day

## Mechanism

The Mongol empire ran a relay post, the yam (also jam, örtöö), founded by Ögedei Khan and described in detail by
Marco Polo for Kublai Khan's China. No single horse or rider covered the distance: post stations stood along every
main road at fixed intervals, each with horses standing ready, so a courier rode one stage flat out and handed over.
For expresses, riders wore belts hung with bells; the next station heard them coming and had a fresh horse and rider
saddled, who took the despatch and galloped on. The riders bound stomach, chest and head with bands to stand the
pace, and carried a tablet (the paiza; Polo's "gerfalcon tablet") that let them take any horse they met if theirs
broke down. The horses cost the Khan nothing: every town and village near a station had to supply its share. A clerk
at each post logged every courier's arrival and departure. The speed came from never letting the letter stop, not
from any fast horse. All the big numbers below are Polo's own figures and the script attributes them to him.

## Claims

- The yam was founded by Ögedei Khan; stations offered spare horses, food and shelter — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_(route)
- Marco Polo wrote that the Khan's messengers find a station called Yamb at every twenty-five miles of the road — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that some stations had four hundred horses standing ready; month by month two hundred were kept at the station and two hundred at grass — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that the posts kept more than three hundred thousand horses and that the station buildings were more than ten thousand in number — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that express riders wore girdles hung with bells, and when those at the next post hear the bells they get ready another horse and man, who takes over the letter — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that these express riders travel two hundred or two hundred and fifty miles in the day, and as much in the night — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that the riders bind hard the stomach, chest and head with strong bands — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that each express rider carries a gerfalcon tablet, and if his horse breaks down he may make whomever he meets on the road dismount and give up his horse; nobody dares refuse — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that every city, village or hamlet near a post station must furnish horses to the post, so the horses cost the Emperor nothing — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that a clerk at each post notes the time of each courier's arrival and departure — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Marco Polo wrote that fruit gathered one morning in Cambaluc (Khanbaliq, the capital) reaches the Great Khan at Chandu (Shangdu) the evening of the next day, a distance of ten days' journey — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636
- Paiza (Mongolian gerege) is the tablet that gave a messenger on the post roads room, board, fresh horses and an escort; the Metropolitan Museum example is iron inlaid with silver, and the inscriptions open "By the power of eternal heaven" — https://rubinmuseum.org/projecthimalayanart/essays/mongol-messengers-badge-paiza-or-gerege-in-pakpa-script/

## Left out

- Modern estimates (stations 20-40 miles apart, 120-190 miles a day): they disagree with Polo's figures, so the
  script speaks only Polo's numbers, attributed to him.
- "1,234 stations", horses-per-station ranges of "20 to several hundred", and gold / silver / wood paiza ranks: only
  low-quality or AI-generated pages give them.
- A Pony Express comparison: no source checked.
- Ögedei's founding year (1234 in some pages): not verified, and the script needs no year.
- Polo's foot runners every three miles (forty houses round a little fort): a second relay that would split the
  story; the bells and the fruit are told about the relay as a whole.
- "Interference was punishable by death": only the general paiza formula ("shall die") is sourced, and Kublai
  dropped that wording; not used.
