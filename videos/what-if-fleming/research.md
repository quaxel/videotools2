# Research: What if Fleming had cleaned up his lab? (discovery of penicillin, 1928)

## Mechanism

In 1928 Alexander Fleming, a bacteriologist at St Mary's Hospital in London, was studying Staphylococcus aureus,
the germ behind boils and, in the blood, sepsis (blood poisoning). In August he went on his summer holiday and left
several freshly inoculated culture plates on a corner of his bench instead of in the incubator. He was back on
3 September 1928; with his former assistant Merlin Pryce he found one plate contaminated by a blue-green mould, and
around the mould the staphylococci had not grown. "That's funny," he said. The mould was releasing a substance that
kills bacteria; he called it penicillin. Penicillin only kills bacteria while they are growing and dividing, so the
mould had to get going before the germs did. Ronald Hare later reproduced the effect only below about 20 °C and
found that late August 1928 had nine cool days; he argued that had the plates gone into a warm incubator the germs
would have grown first and nothing would have happened. Fleming, "a bacteriologist, not a chemist", could not
isolate or purify it. In 1940 Howard Florey's team at Oxford (Ernst Chain, Norman Heatley and others) concentrated
it and showed it cured infected mice, but could only make small amounts; American labs and factories then scaled
it up, to 2.3 million doses in time for the Normandy landings in 1944. Estimates of lives saved by penicillin and the
antibiotics that followed run from about two hundred to five hundred million.

## Claims

- Before penicillin an infected scratch could kill: in December 1940 police constable Albert Alexander was scratched by a rose thorn and was dying of septicaemia when conventional treatments failed — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Heatley
- In nineteen twenty-eight (1928) Alexander Fleming, at St Mary's Hospital in London, was studying Staphylococcus aureus — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- Staphylococcus aureus causes boils and sepsis (blood poisoning) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staphylococcus_aureus
- That August (in August 1928) Fleming went on his summer holiday after inoculating several culture plates, which he left on a corner of his bench (the table), not in the incubator — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- He was back in his laboratory on September third (3 September 1928), where Merlin Pryce greeted him — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- Watching the contaminated plate, Fleming said "That's funny" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- One plate was contaminated with a blue-green mould; around the mould the bacteria did not grow, meaning the mould killed them — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- The mould released a substance that stopped bacterial growth; Fleming named it penicillin — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- Fleming could not isolate (purify) penicillin into a usable drug ("I am a bacteriologist, not a chemist") — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- Twelve years later, in 1940 (nineteen forty, 1928 + 12), Howard Florey's team at Oxford (Ernst Chain, Norman Heatley) made concentrated penicillin that worked in vivo (eight mice, May 1940) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Heatley
- The Oxford team could produce only a small amount (tiny amounts); its first patient's supply ran out — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin
- Then American labs and factories (USDA Peoria laboratory, Merck, Pfizer deep-tank fermentation) scaled it up to mass production — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin
- By D-Day the United States produced 2.3 million doses (over two million doses) in time for the invasion of Normandy in 1944 — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin ; https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2017-07/thanks-to-penicillin-lesson.pdf ; https://news.wisc.edu/?p=38190
- One expert (Ronald Hare, 1970) argued that had Fleming put the cultures in the incubator instead of leaving them on the bench, the effect would not have occurred: penicillin acts only on growing bacteria, so the mould had to grow first (the germs would have grown first), and the mould does well below 20 °C; late August 1928 had nine cool days — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin
- By some estimates penicillin and the antibiotics it inspired have saved hundreds of millions of lives (estimates: more than 200 million, Guinness World Records; more than 500 million, Popular Science) — https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/760568-most-life-saving-fungi ; https://www.popsci.com/science/penicillin-discovery/

## Left out

- The spores-through-the-window story: Fleming's own 1945 suggestion, disputed by Pryce and Hare (the window was kept shut); the likely source was La Touche's lab a floor below. Not used, and the set shows the window shut.
- La Touche, Craddock, Ridley, Paine's 1930 Sheffield cures: true, off-story.
- Albert Alexander dying when supplies ran out: grim; the script only says "tiny amounts".
- Anne Miller (half the US supply on one patient), the Peoria cantaloupe, 646 billion units by 1945, the 1945 Nobel Prize: tempting, but not in the approved script.
- Any single lives-saved figure: estimates range from ~82 million (penicillin alone, Science Heroes) to over 500 million; the script keeps the hedged "by some estimates, hundreds of millions".
- The National WWII Museum lesson-plan PDF: search shows it carries the 2.3 million doses figure, but its text could not be extracted here; the verified wording is from Wikipedia "Penicillin" (and UW-Madison: "millions of doses" by D-Day).
- "Maybe someone else finds it, but not in time for the war" and "maybe someone in your family tree" are speculation, worded as such in the script.
- The dialogue ("That's funny." is sourced; "It works!" is an illustrative re-enactment, not a recorded quote).
