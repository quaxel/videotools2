# Research: Why a cabin pressure loss gives you only seconds of useful consciousness

## Mechanism

At cruise (around thirty-five thousand feet) the outside air is far too thin to keep a person conscious, so an
airliner pumps air in and holds the cabin at a pressure no higher than an eight-thousand-foot altitude in normal
operation (14 CFR 25.841). If the cabin loses pressure, the "cabin altitude" climbs toward the outside altitude; on a
sudden decompression the cooling air can fog the cabin. The passenger masks must be presented automatically before the
cabin altitude passes fifteen thousand feet (14 CFR 25.1447; A320-family cabins open the PSU doors at about fourteen
thousand). The danger is hypoxia: it gives little warning, and one of its symptoms is euphoria, a sense of well-being
(FAA). At thirty-five thousand feet the FAA's average time of useful consciousness, the time you can still act
rationally, is thirty to sixty seconds, and fifteen to thirty after a rapid decompression: a minute at most either way. Pulling the mask toward you pulls a
lanyard that fires a chemical oxygen generator (sodium chlorate and iron powder) which runs for roughly twelve to twenty
minutes and gets hot; the masks are continuous flow, so the reservoir bag may stay flat while oxygen is flowing. The
pilots put on their own masks first and fly an emergency descent to no higher than ten thousand feet, where people can
breathe without supplemental oxygen.

## Claims

- Airliners pressurize the cabin to a cabin pressure altitude of not more than eight thousand feet (8,000 ft) under normal operating conditions — https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/25.841
- Passenger oxygen units must be automatically presented before the cabin pressure altitude exceeds fifteen thousand feet (15,000 ft) — https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/25.1447
- On A320-family aircraft the PSU panels drop open when cabin altitude exceeds about fourteen thousand feet (14,000 ft) — https://planefyi.com/systems/passenger-oxygen-system/
- FAA average time of useful consciousness at thirty-five thousand feet (35,000 ft, FL350) is thirty to sixty seconds (30 to 60 s); after rapid decompression 15 to 30 s — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_of_useful_consciousness
- At thirty-five thousand feet every FAA time-of-useful-consciousness figure is a minute at most (30 to 60 s on ascent, 15 to 30 s after rapid decompression), so "a minute at most" holds for the sudden decompression shown — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_of_useful_consciousness
- Altitude chamber tests show some people in an oxygen-deficient environment experience a sense of euphoria, a feeling of increased well-being; symptoms include tingling, impaired judgment and euphoria — https://www.faasafety.gov/files/events/WP/WP09/2019/WP0992648/hypoxia.pdf
- Immediately after an explosive decompression a heavy fog may fill the cabin as the air cools — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncontrolled_decompression
- Pulling the mask toward you activates the chemical oxygen generator; it contains sodium chlorate and iron powder, runs for about twelve to twenty-two minutes (at least twelve minutes) depending on size, and the canister gets hot — https://planefyi.com/systems/passenger-oxygen-system/
- Passenger masks are continuous flow; "although the bag on the oxygen mask may not inflate, oxygen is flowing to the mask" — https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/67142/why-does-airplane-oxygen-mask-have-bag-may-not-inflate
- After a decompression the pilots first don their own oxygen masks, then commence a rapid descent to an altitude no higher than ten thousand feet (10,000 ft) — https://www.salon.com/2010/11/09/when_planes_depressurize

## Left out

- **Mask oxygen "12 to 15 minutes"**: sources give 12-20 and 12-22 minutes, so the script says "at least twelve minutes", the floor they share.
- **Masks drop at fourteen thousand feet**: aircraft-specific (A320 family). The regulation says before fifteen thousand, so the script uses "before fifteen thousand".
- **"Thirty to sixty seconds"**: the FAA table gives it for a normal ascent at thirty-five thousand feet, but the video shows a sudden decompression (fifteen to thirty seconds). The sources disagree on the case shown, so the script says "a minute at most", which every row supports.
- **Blue lips, tunnel vision**: real hypoxia symptoms, but the story needs only one, the euphoria.
- **Real accidents (Helios 522, Southwest 1380, Alaska 1282)**: dramatic, but naming them would turn the explainer into a crash story and add facts that need their own sourcing.
- **"Normal cruise cabin altitude is 6,000 to 8,000 ft"**: the regulation gives only the 8,000 ft ceiling, so the script says "eight thousand".
