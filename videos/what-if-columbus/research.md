# Research: What if Columbus's crew had turned back? (October 1492)

## Mechanism

On 6 September 1492 Columbus's three small ships, the nao Santa María and the caravels Pinta and Niña, left San
Sebastián de La Gomera in the Canary Islands and sailed west. His journal survives only as an abstract made by
Bartolomé de las Casas, a Dominican friar, from a copy. In it, from Sunday 9 September, Columbus records two
reckonings of the distance: the true one, and a shorter one announced to the crew "that the crew might not be
dismayed" (some modern scholars, Dunn and Kelley, read the two sets as a unit confusion rather than a deception;
the script keeps Las Casas's reading and hedges it as known only from a friar's summary). On Wednesday 10 October
"the men lost all patience, and complained of the length of the voyage"; the abstract says the Admiral encouraged
them. Later writers add a bargain: Oviedo, who talked with participants, says Columbus promised to turn back if land
was not found within three days. On 11 October the crews fished out signs of land: a stick that seemed carved with
an iron tool and a stalk loaded with rose berries. At about two hours after midnight on 12 October Rodrigo de Triana,
a sailor on the Pinta, sighted land and the Pinta fired the signal. Columbus said he had seen a light at ten o'clock
the evening before, and claimed the crown's promised annuity of ten thousand maravedís for the first sighting.
Had they turned back, Atlantic crossings were coming anyway: a Portuguese fleet under Pedro Álvares Cabral reached
Brazil in April 1500. The crossing began the Columbian exchange (Nunn & Qian): potatoes, maize and cacao went to the
Old World; smallpox, measles and other Old World diseases went to the Americas, where, with war, enslavement and
forced labour, the Indigenous population fell by an estimated 80-95 percent.

## Left out
- Dunn and Kelley's view that the two sets of distances were Columbus's leagues against the crew's Portuguese leagues, not a deception: a real scholarly dispute. The script keeps Las Casas's reading and hedges the log as "known only from a friar's summary"; the hook "lied" follows the abstract's own words.
- The league figures of each day (e.g. 9-10 September): not needed, and numbers on screen would need claims. The logbooks show words only (TRUE / FOR THE CREW).
- Morison dates the three-day promise to 9 October and other accounts give "two or three days": the script says only "Some later writers say ... three more days".
- Fernando Colón's account of the bargain: not confirmed in a source we could open, so not cited.
- Whether Cabral's landfall was intentional ("perhaps intentionally"): disputed; the script says only that Portugal reached Brazil in 1500.
- Martín Alonso Pinzón's bonus and the Pinta's cannon signal: true, but side tracks.
- Exact population totals (pre-contact estimates range widely): only "most" is spoken; the screen shows no figure.
- The name San Salvador / Guanahaní and the 12 October landing: not needed for the turning-back question.
