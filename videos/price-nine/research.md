# Research: Why prices always end in 9

## Mechanism

People read a price from left to right and give the leftmost digit the most weight, so a price one cent lower that
changes the left digit ($2.99 instead of $3.00) feels clearly smaller (Thomas and Morwitz, 2005). Retailers use the
9-ending because it moves demand more than the cent costs them: in a women's clothing catalog test by Anderson and
Simester, the same item sold almost equally at $44 and $54 but significantly more at $49. The same left-digit bias
shows up far from the shelf: in more than 22 million wholesale used-car sales, prices dropped by about $150 to $200 at
every 10,000-mile odometer threshold (Lacetera, Pope and Sydnor).

## Claims

- Thomas and Morwitz (2005, two thousand five), Journal of Consumer Research: nine-ending prices are perceived lower than a price one cent higher when the leftmost digit changes, e.g. $3.00 (three dollars) reduced by one cent to $2.99 (two ninety-nine) — https://phys.org/news/2005-06-consumers-react-prices.html
- Anderson and Simester, women's clothing catalog: prices of $44, $49 and $54 (forty-four, forty-nine and fifty-four dollars) for the same item; demand was almost identical at $44 and $54 but significantly higher at $49 — https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/yours-only-1999
- Lacetera, Pope and Sydnor: wholesale auctions with more than 22 million (twenty-two million) used car transactions — https://www.nber.org/digest/sep11/limited-attention-car-market
- Price drops at each 10,000-mile (ten thousand miles) threshold from 10,000 to 100,000 miles, ranging from about $150 to $200 (one hundred fifty to two hundred dollars) — https://www.nber.org/digest/sep11/limited-attention-car-market

Notes: the catalog test is often retold as $34/$39/$44; the source we opened gives $44/$49/$54, so the script uses
those. The popular story that 9-endings were invented to stop cashiers stealing is not sourced and is left out, as is
any "share of prices ending in 9" figure.
