# Research: What if Petrov had reported the launch? (1983 Soviet nuclear false alarm)

## Mechanism

Shortly after midnight on 26 September 1983, the Soviet Oko early-warning satellites reported one American
intercontinental missile launch, then four more. The duty officer at the Serpukhov-15 command bunker near Moscow,
Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, was supposed to notify his superiors of any incoming attack. Three weeks after
the Soviets shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007, Moscow feared an American first strike. Petrov reasoned that a real
first strike would come as hundreds of simultaneous launches, not five, and ground radar could not confirm anything,
so he reported it as a system false alarm. No missiles arrived. The alarm was later traced to sunlight glinting off
high-altitude clouds, lined up with the satellites' Molniya orbits. Petrov said he got no reward but a reprimand over
paperwork; the incident stayed secret until 1998. Historians disagree about the counterfactual: some (Podvig, the
Russian mission) say several more decision layers and confirmation from other systems were required before any launch;
others (Bruce Blair) say the leadership, with only a couple of minutes, would have retaliated. Petrov later said he was
"50/50".

## Left out

- Whether he actually phoned superiors at all: accounts differ, so the script only quotes his own "report a system fault" decision as a reconstruction.
- North Dakota as the cloud location: only some sources give it; not needed.
- "Saved the world" as a fact: contested (Podvig); the script hedges with "some say".
- The 269 dead of KAL 007: true but grim and not needed for the story.
- Dresden Peace Prize, the 2014 film: off-story.
- An exact decision window in minutes: Blair's "couple of minutes" is one person's estimate; the script says only "minutes".
- The dialogue lines ("Comrade Petrov!", "Still nothing, sir.") are illustrative re-enactment, not recorded quotes.
